Date: 2026-11-01<br>
Baseline: `ff496fa328b73c4fba90053688c80dad5c685e4e`<br>

## Research reviewed

The review used the bodies of the sources below, not search-result snippets.

- The PhET team’s [empirical design paper](https://phet.colorado.edu/publications/archive/Phet%20Interview%20Paper.htm) reports think-aloud testing of simulation interfaces. It emphasizes intuitive controls and representations, immediate useful feedback, and the measurable learning cost of even small amounts of irrelevant information.
- The PhET team’s [minimal-guidance paper](https://phet.colorado.edu/publications/Paulson_etal_2009/Paulson_etal_2009.pdf) compares more directed and less directed simulation activities. A concise driving question left room for productive exploration, while step-by-step prompts could duplicate guidance already supplied by the simulation.
- Mayer and Moreno’s [Nine Ways to Reduce Cognitive Load in Multimedia Learning](https://carpentries.github.io/instructor-training/files/papers/mayer-reduce-cognitive-load-2003.pdf) explains the limited capacity of working memory and distinguishes essential processing from incidental processing and representational holding. The practical consequence here is to keep the prediction, changed variable, evidence, and explanation together while removing repeated framing.
- Schneider et al.’s [signaling meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.05.003) synthesizes 103 studies with 12,201 participants and reports positive effects for retention and transfer. Signaling should expose the structure of the comparison, not add more prose; this informed the numbered challenges, state labels, and consistent `actual% · expected %` pattern.
- Hattie and Timperley’s [The Power of Feedback](https://educacion.udd.cl/files/2018/04/The-Power-of-Feedback.pdf) finds that useful feedback is tied to the task or process and helps close the gap between the learner’s current and intended understanding. Personal praise and unrelated detail are weak substitutes. Chance feedback therefore names the observed counts, evaluates the prediction, and supplies the probability reason.
- Illustrative Mathematics’ [Grade 7 Unit 8, Lesson 2](https://cms-assets.illustrativemathematics.org/6irkoai64y659j10nbmb88g4u5b0) uses a short goal, an experiment, relative frequency, and explanation. Its [Rolling Twice](https://tasks.illustrativemathematics.org/content-standards/tasks/890) task recommends comparing experimental and theoretical results and says more runs are _likely_ to be closer, not guaranteed to be closer. The [7.SP.C.7 standard page](https://tasks.illustrativemathematics.org/content-standards/7/SP/C/7) likewise asks learners to compare model probabilities with observed frequencies and explain discrepancies.
- OpenStax’s [probability terminology](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics/pages/3-1-terminology) distinguishes observed relative frequency from theoretical probability and describes long-run convergence as a tendency, not exact agreement in every sample. Its [independent-events section](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics/pages/3-2-independent-and-mutually-exclusive-events) defines independence as one event not affecting the chance of another.

## Applied editorial rules

1. Start with one driving question, then move directly to prediction, manipulation, evidence, and explanation.
2. Let controls label themselves. Remove prose that merely repeats a button, select, or slider.
3. Show evidence in the same form the learner must compare: `38.0% · expected 40.0%`. Do not expose a derived “gap” score and do not relabel percentage-point differences as percentages.
4. Keep feedback specific to the learner’s prediction and the actual result. State the reason, not generic praise.
5. Signal the controlled variable explicitly: chances stay at 25%; only sample size changes.
6. State the larger-sample result accurately: larger samples usually settle nearer expected shares, but one particular larger sample can be farther away.
7. State independence once near the recent draws: a streak does not change the next draw.
8. Keep model limitations that affect interpretation: computer draws are not physical spins or rolls, a finite sample cannot prove fairness, and weights do not model a physically loaded die.
9. Keep supporting catalog prose for search and non-interactive contexts, but do not render it again beneath the complete Chance activity.
10. Use `%` everywhere in Chance learner copy. `pp`, “percentage points,” and the visible gap metric are removed.

Registry-only copy was also shortened for catalog/search/native fallbacks:

- Explanation: expected chance is weight ÷ total weight; observed share comes from one sample; equal weights do not require equal counts; zero weight is impossible; larger samples usually settle nearer expected shares but one can be farther away; draws are independent.
- Experiment 1: “Compare sample sizes”; run 12 and 1,200 trials with four equal weights; compare observed percentages with 25% expected.
- Experiment 2: “Double one weight”; choose 40% (2 of 5) or 50% (2 of 4), then run 200 trials; compare observed percentage with 40% expected.
- Misconception: past draws do not make an outcome “due.”
- Source titles: “Illustrative Mathematics: Models and observed frequencies”, “OpenStax: Long-run relative frequency”, and “OpenStax: Independent events”.

## Measured visible word reduction

Measurement scope is the rendered `.discovery-detail` `innerText`: the Chance detail heading, activity, feedback for the named state, and Sources disclosure. It excludes the global Family Manager navigation because that copy is not owned by the lesson. Counts use Unicode letter/number tokens with internal apostrophes or hyphens kept as one word. The same deterministic fixture and `Math.random` seed (`0x51a7c0de`) were used before and after.

| State                             | Before | After |           Reduction |
| --------------------------------- | -----: | ----: | ------------------: |
| Initial                           |    654 |   180 |   474 words (72.5%) |
| Both challenges completed         |    826 |   294 |   532 words (64.4%) |
| Expanded controls, initial sample |    735 |   231 |   504 words (68.6%) |
| Three-state total                 |  2,215 |   705 | 1,510 words (68.2%) |

Desktop 1440×1000 and mobile 390×844 produce the same text counts because the responsive layout changes placement, not lesson copy. Counting both viewports would double every numerator and denominator without changing the 68.2% reduction, so the table reports the three distinct text states rather than inflating the sample.

These figures are deliberately scoped. They do not count the non-rendered catalog explanation, accessibility-only descriptions, native React Native view, or independent package frame because those surfaces do not share one comparable browser `innerText` boundary. Their strings are nevertheless included in the inventory above and covered by focused tests/builds.
